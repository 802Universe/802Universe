RADAR & DFS - WHY IT MATTERS TO 802.11#

The 5 GHz UNII-2A (5250-5350 MHz, ch 52-64) and UNII-2C/2-Extended (5470-5725 MHz, ch 100-144) bands are shared with radar as the PRIMARY user. Wi-Fi is SECONDARY and must yield. IEEE 802.11h + regional rules (FCC 47 CFR 15.407, ETSI EN 301 893) require every device on these channels to run DFS: detect radar, stop transmitting, and move - protecting weather, air-traffic, military and maritime radar.

Escalation relevance: an unexplained mass client disconnect, a channel that silently changes, or an AP that "goes quiet for a minute at boot" on ch 52-144 is very often DFS - not a Wi-Fi bug. This sheet lets you tell a real DFS event from a false trigger, and map a detected waveform back to the radar that caused it. 6 GHz has NO DFS, which is a key reason to move high-density traffic there.

REAL RADAR vs FALSE DFS TRIGGER - HOW TO TELL#

FALSE-POSITIVE sources (common): poorly-shielded microwave ovens (2.4 GHz leak rarely, but some 5 GHz industrial heaters), certain FHSS/wireless-video/cordless bridges, non-compliant outdoor links, perimeter/security radar, and adjacent AP self-detection. A false trigger looks identical to a real one to the regulator - the AP must evacuate either way.

Signs it is a REAL radar: (1) correlates with a physical source - within ~35 km of an airport (ASR/TDWR), coast/port (marine), or a weather-radar site (NEXRAD); (2) repeats on the SAME channels that map to that radar in the catalogue above; (3) periodicity matches a rotation (e.g. hits every ~4.5 s = ASR sweep, or bursts consistent with TDWR).

Signs it may be FALSE: hits on random DFS channels with no nearby radar source; strongly correlated with a specific piece of on-site equipment powering on; only one AP in a cluster ever triggers (localized RF source). Enhanced/Enterprise DFS (EDFS) and vendor "radar signatures" reduce but do not eliminate these.

Data to collect for an escalation: AP model + regulatory domain, exact channel(s) and time of each DFS event, whether it was CAC-time or in-service, the vendor radar-type classification if logged (maps to the FCC/ETSI type tables above), site geography (airport/coast/weather-radar proximity), and whether one AP or many detected it.
